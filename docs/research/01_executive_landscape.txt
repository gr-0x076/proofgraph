Executive Summary
ProofGraph—an AI platform that atomically decomposes documents into verifiable claims, retrieves
evidence, classifies support/contradiction/insufficiency, preserves provenance, and models claim-source
relationships in a graph—sits at the intersection of five mature research streams and several nascent
ones. No single prior system combines all these components identically, but each component has
substantial prior art. This report maps the complete landscape: what is established, what is
implemented, what is genuinely difficult, and where defensible opportunities remain as of November
2026.
Key findings for decision-makers:
1. The core pipeline (decompose retrieve classify) is well-established research. The novelty is
integration: graph-structured provenance, explicit disagreement modeling, and production-grade
explainability together are underserved by existing tools.
2. Retrieval quality is the dominant performance bottleneck—replacing retrieved evidence with gold
evidence improves LLM accuracy by 14–22 percentage points [18].
3. Claim decomposition helps weaker verifiers but can hurt stronger ones; it is not a universally
beneficial step [76].
4. Benchmark leakage (parametric and temporal) is a serious, partially unresolved threat to claimed

Section 1: Research Systems, Benchmarks, and Datasets
1.1 Core Benchmarks
FEVER (Fact Extraction and VERification) [1] remains the foundational benchmark. It contains 185,445
claims derived from Wikipedia sentences and labeled Supported, Refuted, or NotEnoughInfo (NEI), with
gold evidence sentences recorded for the first two classes. Introduced at NAACL-HLT 2018 by Thorne et
al. [1], it established the standard three-way classification and the pipeline of document retrieval
sentence extraction verdict prediction. Its critical limitation: claims are generated by mutating
Wikipedia sentences, so models can exploit claim-language bias and achieve good performance without
using evidence [14].
FEVEROUS [3] extends FEVER to 87,026 claims requiring evidence from both unstructured text and
structured table cells from Wikipedia. It introduces challenge types (Numerical Reasoning, Multi-hop,
Entity Disambiguation, Combining Tables and Text), making it more realistic but harder for uniform
models. Evidence is limited to three evidence sets per claim [3]. The best multi-stage retrieval pipeline
(M-ReRank) achieves 93.63% Wikipedia page recall at development set but only ~10% evidence
precision [81], illustrating the precision-recall gap at the retrieval stage.
AVeriTeC [2][4] is the leading real-world benchmark: 4,568 claims from 50 fact-checking organizations,
labeled Supported, Refuted, Not Enough Evidence, or Conflicting Evidence/Cherry-picking, with
question-answer pairs, textual justifications, speaker/publisher/date metadata, and web evidence. The
2025 shared task introduced a new scoring metric (Ev2R recall, minimum threshold 0.44 to count a
verdict correct) and enforced hard reproducibility constraints: open-weights only, single GPU with 23GB
RAM, 60 seconds per claim average, no proprietary LLMs [4][10]. The winning system (CTU AIC) scored
33.17% [10]; the SANCTUARY pipeline placed third at 25.27% [43]. An important design note: the
AVeriTeC 2025 test set was temporally separated (train/dev claims up to 2019, test claims from 2024
onward), making it a strict out-of-distribution temporal evaluation [43].
EX-FEVER [12][13] adds multi-hop explainability: over 60,000 claims with 2-hop and 3-hop reasoning
from hyperlinked Wikipedia documents, each with a veracity label and human-annotated natural
language explanation of the reasoning path. Its baseline uses TF-IDF + neural retrieval (MDR), BART for
explanation generation, and BERT/GEAR for verdict prediction [13]. With perfect gold explanations, BERT
reaches 99.29% accuracy, suggesting explanation quality is the bottleneck [12]. Limitation:
Wikipedia-only sourcing and ROUGE as the only explanation metric, which may not capture reasoning
quality [12].
SciFact [17] covers scientific claim verification: 1,109 claims against a corpus of biomedical abstracts,
with rationale sentences labeled Supported or Contradicted. The pipeline uses rationale selection
(RoBERTa-large) followed by label prediction (fine-tuned on FEVER+SciFact). MultiVerS is the current
state-of-the-art [17]. SciFact is important for ProofGraph because scientific domains require specialized

ClaimVer [47][52] is both a system and an implicit benchmark: it verifies claims against Wikidata using
multi-node BFS (Woolnet, up to 3 hops), labels as Attributable/Extrapolatory/Contradictory, and
introduces a Knowledge Graph Attribution Score (KAS) [47]. Solar-10.7B-Chat achieved >89% accuracy on
its custom WikiQA-derived dataset [47]. The explainability design—highlighting individual claims in a
document with per-claim KG evidence—is architecturally close to ProofGraph's report generation goal.
CONFACT [29][78] addresses a gap: fact-checking with conflicting evidence. Built from 3,180 claims (566
AVeriTeC + 2,614 FactCheckQA), 17.8% exhibited conflicting evidence from sources of varying
credibility [29]. Human annotators overestimated mainstream news reliability (95.8% rated
credible/neutral) while MBFC flagged 30% of misleading sources that annotators trusted [29].
Inter-annotator agreement (Krippendorff's α 0.586) reflects inherent human uncertainty on conflicting
evidence [29].
VERITAS [82] is the newest development (ACL 2026): a dynamic benchmark of 25,000 real-world
multimodal claims from 104 organizations across 54 languages, updated quarterly to prevent leakage. It
decomposes claim assessment into five dimensions (media authenticity, media contextualization, claim
veracity, context coverage, integrity) each scored 1 to +1, evaluated with MSE rather than
accuracy [82]. The ensemble of four frontier LLMs (GPT-5.2, Gemini 2.5 Pro, Claude Sonnet 4.5, Llama 4
Maverick) achieves MSE 0.034 against human annotations [82]. This is the most rigorous anti-leakage
benchmark design currently available.
CheckThat! / CLEF [28][77] is an ongoing shared task series. The 2026 edition has three tasks: (1)
source retrieval for scientific web claims, (2) fact-checking numerical and temporal claims with reasoning
trace ranking, (3) generating full fact-checking articles [77]. Task 2 uses test-time scaling (multiple LLM
traces at varying temperatures, de-duplicated, ranked by a verifier) [77]. The 2025 numerical claim task
used BM25 over 17,000+ claims across English/Spanish/Arabic, with severe class imbalance (~58% False,
18% True, 24% Conflicting) [28].
FACTS Grounding [65] (Google DeepMind) benchmarks LLM grounding against provided input
documents, with 1,719 examples covering finance, technology, retail, medicine, law, evaluated by an
ensemble of three frontier LLM judges (Gemini 1.5 Pro, GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet). The public/private
split design protects against leakage [65].
FactIR [79] is a retrieval-focused benchmark from Factiverse production logs: 100 real-world claims,
90,047 corpus documents, 1,413 annotated claim-evidence pairs, zero-shot setting. BM25 and SPLADEv2
surprisingly outperform classical dense retrievers (DPR, Contriever) on this benchmark [79], with the
best model (snowflake-arctic-embed-s) reaching nDCG@5 0.367 [79].
Survey resources: The TACL 2022 survey "A Survey on Automated Fact-Checking" and its EMNLP 2023
multimodal follow-up, curated in the Cartus repository [9], provide the most comprehensive cataloguing
through late 2024. The 2024 survey on generative LLMs in automated fact-checking [8] covers 199
papers using PRISMA methodology and identifies multilingual and low-resource fact-checking as

Section 2: Retrieval Methods
2.1 Sparse and Lexical Retrieval
BM25 remains a strong baseline despite its age. It is used as the first retrieval stage in FEVER [1], the
2024 AVeriTeC baseline [43], ClaimCheck [7], LOKI [22], and many others. Critically, on the FactIR
real-world benchmark, BM25 and SPLADEv2 outperform several neural dense retrievers [79]. On
ClimateCheck and ClimateFEVER, TF-IDF consistently outperforms BM25, possibly because BM25's
document-length normalization penalizes long passages [18]. A 2021 study found BM25 outperforms
neural retrieval on the majority of 18 out-of-domain IR benchmarks [18]. TF-IDF achieves within 5% of
fine-tuned transformers across 29 climate-related NLP benchmarks [18].
SPLADE-v3 (a sparse neural retriever) is used in hybrid fusion pipelines; in the ClimateCheck2025 system
it is combined with BM25 and BGE-M3 via Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF, k=60) [24].

2.2 Dense Retrieval
Dense retrieval with FAISS inner-product indexing over sentence embeddings is used in DeReC [25], the
FACT5 pipeline [11], and the journalist augmentation system [62]. Common embedding models include
all-MiniLM-L6-v2 [11][58], bilingual-embedding-small, and snowflake-arctic-embed-m-v2.0 [43]. HippoRAG
builds KG-based long-term memory using Personalized PageRank over dense-retrieved entity
graphs [35]. DPR and Contriever underperform BM25 on domain-shifted real-world fact-checking
data [79].
Multi-hop dense retrieval (MDR) is used in EX-FEVER baselines for iterative evidence accumulation across
document hops [12][13]. ColBERT/SPLADE are mentioned as advanced retrievers in the ClimateCheck
hybrid system [24]; ColBERTv2 is listed as a FactIR component [79].

2.3 Hybrid Retrieval and Reranking
The best-performing retrieval pipelines are consistently hybrid. The ClimateCheck2025 system fuses
BM25 + fine-tuned BGE-M3 (dense) + SPLADE-v3 (sparse neural) via RRF to select top-600 candidates,
then applies a cross-encoder reranker (ms-marco-MiniLM-L-6-v2) with two-phase hard negative mining,
raising Recall@10 from 0.384 to 0.564 [24]. Adding an LLM-based permutation reranker
(RankGPT/GPT-4.1) further raises Recall@10 to 0.601 [24].
The M-ReRank pipeline for FEVEROUS combines TF-IDF, BM25, Cross-Encoder, HybRank (sparse-dense
collaborative filtering via RoBERTa), and HLATR, achieving 93.63% Wikipedia page recall [81].
SANCTUARY's two-step system uses BM25 over 500-token windows for coarse retrieval (top 125 chunks),
then semantic chunking with near-duplicate filtering for fine-grained retrieval [43].
Reranking in the numerical claim task (CheckThat! 2025) did not improve over raw BM25 [28], illustrating
domain dependency.

2.4 Web Search and Iterative Retrieval
ClaimCheck, LOKI, FIRE, and Veracity all use live web search (Serper/Google Search API) with temporal
cutoff filtering to avoid post-claim evidence [7][22][44][80]. FIRE's iterative architecture uses a unified
decision module: either output a verdict (when confident) or generate a new search query and

FAISS (Meta) is the most widely used [11][25][62]. ChromaDB is noted as an alternative [11][67].
Elasticsearch BM25 is used in FactIR [79]. Pinecone, Weaviate, and others are supported by the
Haystack framework [70]. All are suitable for ProofGraph's evidence store component.

Section 3: Claim Detection and Atomic Decomposition
3.1 Atomic Decomposition
FActScore [5][16] is the foundational system: it breaks long-form text into atomic facts and measures
the percentage supported by a reliable knowledge source. ChatGPT achieved only 58% FActScore on
biographies [5]. It is available as a pip package, costing ~$1/100 sentences via OpenAI API [16]. The
knowledge source is a 2023 Wikipedia dump; building the database takes ~8 hours [16]. FActScore
measures precision only; the number of facts per response measures recall [16].
Claimify [6] (Microsoft Research, ACL 2025) is the state-of-the-art claim extraction system. It operates in
four stages: sentence splitting with context creation selection disambiguation decomposition. It
accepts question-answer pairs as input. Key claims: 99% of extracted claims are entailed by their source
sentence; it best balances verifiable vs. unverifiable content; it is least likely to omit context critical to
the fact-checking verdict [6]. Crucially, Claimify is the first system to identify when source text has
multiple possible interpretations and abstain from extraction when interpretation is ambiguous [6].
Limitation: research-only, not commercially available [6]. Benchmarked against VeriScore, DnD, SAFE,
AFaCTA, and Factcheck-GPT [6].
VeriScore [53] extracts only verifiable claims from long-form text, distinguishing it from FActScore which
treats all claims as verifiable. It evaluates 16 models; GPT-4o is best, with Mixtral-8x22B closing the
gap [53]. Factuality scores do not correlate across tasks (e.g., biography vs. long-form QA) [53], an
important caution for ProofGraph system design.
VeriFastScore [49] fine-tunes Llama3.1 8B to jointly extract and verify all claims in a single pass against
Google Search evidence, achieving 6.6× speedup over VeriScore. Pearson correlation with VeriScore:
~0.87 [49]. Limitation: relies on synthetic supervision from VeriScore labels; retrieval noise from
sentence-level Google queries; exact-match scoring is brittle (23.7% exact vs. 71.6% with
paraphrase-aware judging) [49].
SAFE [55] (Search-Augmented Factuality Evaluator, Google) decomposes responses into individual facts
and verifies each via multi-step Google Search reasoning. It agrees with human annotators 72% of the

ClaimBuster [64] is the pioneering check-worthiness detection tool: a machine learning model (SVM

journalists [64].
Document-level decomposition: LLM-CED [63] uses a single LLM across four stages (core-claim
extraction, ambiguous-unit identification, QA-driven decontextualization, self-reflective review),
achieving chrF 33.52% on AVeriTeC-DCE [63]. The key insight is that decontextualization—making claims
self-contained—is as important as decomposition itself.
Multimodal claim extraction: MMCE [74] is a new benchmark (732 social media image-text posts) for
extracting claims from multimodal content. MLLMs with image-text input outperform text-only across all
metrics [74]. Entailment-based evaluation showed only moderate agreement with humans
(Krippendorff's α 0.59 for reference alignment, 0.07 for entailment) [74], revealing measurement
difficulty.
Decomposition Dilemmas [76]: a critical negative result. Decomposition benefits weaker verifiers
(AlignScore) but tends to hurt stronger ones (Minicheck, GPT-4o-mini). Error categories: omission of
context, ambiguity (vague pronouns), over-decomposition (redundancy), and meaning alteration [76].
FActScore-style decomposition produces more over-decomposition errors; VeriScore-style produces more
context omission [76]. The optimal sub-claim count is at most equal to the input complexity level [76].

Section 4: Evidence Verification, NLI, and Classification
4.1 NLI Models and Classification
The standard three-way label schema (Supported/Contradicted/NEI or Entailment/Contradiction/Neutral)
originates in FEVER [1] and is used across all major benchmarks. NLI-pretrained models (DeBERTa,
RoBERTa) are mapped directly: entailment Supported, contradiction Refuted, neutral NEI [28].
Key negative result on NLI: RefNLI [31] demonstrates that fine-tuned NLI models and few-shot
prompted LLMs fail to recognize context mismatch between premise and hypothesis—when evidence
refers to a different entity than the claim, models still predict false entailment or contradiction [31].
Human annotator agreement on this 1,143-pair benchmark reaches Fleiss' κ 0.83 [31]. Standard NLI
training assumes reference determinacy (premise and hypothesis refer to the same context), which does
not hold when evidence is retrieved from web search [31]. Filtering training examples by low lexical
overlap provides only marginal improvement [31].

Reference (In-)Determinacy: Off-the-shelf RoBERTa NLI predicts far more contradictions against

Verifier performance on benchmarks: Near-human balanced accuracy is reported on aggregated
claim-verification benchmarks (LLMAggreFact, SciFact, FEVER) [66], but analysis of 24,100 GPT-4o-mini
reasoning traces reveals that 49.8% of patterns are simple "direct extraction and matching," with only
0.3% genuine step-by-step verification and 10.4% multi-sentence synthesis [66]. High benchmark scores
primarily reflect retrieval-plus-entailment ability, not robust verification reasoning [66].
MiniCheck (Bespoke-MiniCheck-7B) and AlignScore-large are commonly used as verifiers in
decompose-then-verify pipelines [76]. GraphCheck [27] uses GNN-extracted knowledge graphs as soft
prompts to LLMs, achieving 71.1% balanced accuracy across seven benchmarks with up to 7.1%
improvement over base models [27].

4.2 Source Credibility
CONFACT [29][78] is the primary resource here: LLMs augmented with source credibility information at
both retrieval and generation stages significantly improve fact-checking when evidence conflicts [78].
Annotators systematically overestimate mainstream news credibility [29], and the MBFC database (Media
Bias Fact Check) is used as the credibility reference. This is an underexplored component for
ProofGraph: most systems ignore source credibility entirely.
Veracity [80] shows sources with their documented credibility scores from a predefined database,
providing a user-visible credibility layer. Factiverse [69] claims to rank sources by credibility using signals
from reputable news media and certified fact-checkers.

4.3 Temporal Claims
ChronoFact [41][42] addresses timeline-based temporal verification: it identifies events from both claim
and evidence, organizes them into chronological timelines, and predicts both the veracity of individual
events and their chronological accuracy. The ChronoClaims benchmark has 40,249 training + 3,735 test
claims from Wikidata [41]. 82% of ChronoFact errors involve claims with implicit temporal
information [41]. Prior work (NMT, TemporalFC) was limited to structured data (tables, KGs); ChronoFact
handles natural language [41].
CheckThat! 2026 Task 2 explicitly adds temporal claims to numerical claim verification [77], and
OpenFC enforces strict temporal cutoffs in its web search environment [40].

4.4 Uncertainty and Abstention
AbstentionBench [39] covers 20 datasets for LLM abstention across five categories: unknown answers,
underspecification, false premises, subjective questions, and outdated information. Critical findings: (1)
reasoning fine-tuning degrades abstention by ~24% on average; (2) model scale has almost no effect on
abstention; (3) RLVR post-training worsens abstention; (4) models often hallucinate missing context even
while their reasoning chains express uncertainty [39]. A carefully crafted system prompt can boost
abstention but does not resolve the fundamental inability [39].
FACT5 [11] introduces an UNVERIFIABLE abstention class as an explicit output for the ordinal
fact-checking task, using it when evidence is insufficient. The pipeline reports 71.9% MSE reduction at
pass@3 with Mistral-7B [11].

Section 5: Knowledge Graphs, Provenance, and Graph-Based
Approaches
5.1 Knowledge Graphs for Fact Verification
FactKG [46][51] establishes KG-based fact verification over DBpedia, with five reasoning types. Simple
one-step subgraph retrieval (all triples containing any claim entity) outperforms complex trained
retrieval approaches [14].
ClaimVer [47][52] applies Wikidata BFS retrieval (Woolnet, 3-hop limit) to verify document-level claims,
labeling each as Attributable/Extrapolatory/Contradictory with KG evidence citations. The KAS score
aggregates triplet match scores with higher penalty for contradictory information [47]. This is the most
complete prior implementation of a ProofGraph-style claim-KG verification layer.
EVOCA [32] builds AMR (Abstract Meaning Representation) sub-graphs over high-similarity
claim-evidence token pairs (BERT cosine similarity threshold 0.7), converts them back to text, and passes
to lightweight NLI models. Tested on Climate-BERT-fact-checking data [32]. It uses the Mehlhorn Steiner
tree algorithm (via NetworkX) to extract compact sub-graphs [32].
GraphCheck [27] uses Claude-3.5-Sonnet to extract KG triples from documents and processes them with
GNNs as soft prompts to LLMs, enabling fact-checking of long documents in a single inference call. It
achieves up to 10.5% improvement over ACUEval, 3% over MiniCheck, and 6% over GraphEval [27].
Limitation: no reliable method exists for evaluating extracted KG quality, and gains on longer claims
(AggreFact-CNN) are negative [27].
GraphRAG [50] (Microsoft) extracts knowledge graphs from raw text, builds community hierarchies via
Leiden clustering, generates summaries, and uses these for RAG queries. It offers Global, Local, DRIFT,
and Basic search modes [50]. Claimify was used to evaluate GraphRAG's answer comprehensiveness and
diversity, showing GraphRAG outperforms standard RAG [6]. GraphRAG is an important infrastructure
component for ProofGraph's knowledge layer.
CLAIRE [71] is an agentic ReAct-style system for detecting corpus-level contradictions in Wikipedia. It
estimates ~3.3% of English Wikipedia facts contradict other corpus content (99% CI: 1.6%–5.0%) [71].
About 7.3% of FEVEROUS claims labeled Supported/Refuted involve corpus-level inconsistencies [71],
challenging the assumption that any corpus is internally consistent. This is a critical insight for
ProofGraph: even a trusted knowledge base may contain internal contradictions.
HippoRAG [35] builds a KG as long-term memory with Personalized PageRank for multi-hop retrieval,
but records no provenance distinction between enrichment methods [35].
CORRECT [23] builds a three-layer evidence graph (evidence, context, reference layers) with intra- and
cross-layer reasoning, and uses evidence-conditioned prompt embeddings [23]. Tested on FEVEROUS-S,
BearFact, Check-COVID, and SciFact [23].

W3C PROV-DM [19] is the authoritative standard for provenance modeling. It defines entities, activities,
agents, and core relations (Generation, Usage, Communication, Derivation, Attribution, Association,
Delegation). It is a W3C Recommendation (stable), appropriate as the formal backbone for ProofGraph's

PROV-CRED [35] extends PROV-O with enrichment method subclasses (HumanEnrichment,
DeterministicEnrichment, HeuristicEnrichment, ModelEnrichment), a provcred:Model class (identity,
version, task type, benchmark performance), and a ReliabilityAttribution [35]. Open problem:
confidence scores from KGE models, LLMs, and heuristics are not on a common scale; no standard
cross-method calibration dataset exists [35]. LLMs that score their own triples exhibit correlated errors
(self-evaluation bias) [35].
RDF-Star (forthcoming in RDF 1.2) provides syntactically clean triple-level annotation without reification,
but defines no semantics for the annotations [35]. RDF 1.2 will make ProofGraph's provenance layer
more implementable at triple granularity.
Provenance-Enhanced Statements [34] introduces the DEC framework, which interprets provenance
predicates as epistemic stance indicators and groups provenance-homogeneous statements into
cognitive worlds using doxastic/epistemic modal logics [34]. The prototype reasoner is implemented on
Apache Jena/Fuseki [34]. This is academically rigorous but may be over-specified for a first production
system.
Wikidata's approach [34]: reified statements with provenance, qualifiers, and ranks
(preferred/normal/deprecated), where a statement can be promoted to factual by asserting it again as
an unreified triple. This is a practical reference implementation.

5.3 Claim-Evidence Graph Modeling and Contradiction Links
The CONFACT pipeline models source credibility as a node attribute in the retrieval graph [29][78].
EVOCA aligns claim and evidence AMR graphs [32]. FactKG uses KG paths as evidence structures [46].
The hybrid pipeline in [20] models a one-hop KG retrieval layer with web fallback, using entity linking
(ReFinED) and cross-encoder scoring (MiniLM) for triple relevance [20].
No prior system has explicitly modeled contradiction links as first-class graph edges between claims
and sources, or between competing sources—this is a genuinely underserved design element of
ProofGraph.

Section 6: LLM Pipeline Engineering
6.1 Structured Outputs
Structured JSON output is "well-supported mainly in GPT-4" [22]; switching to Llama-3 can cause pipeline
failures without specific design [22]. LOKI wraps all LLM calls with a retry mechanism and traffic
queue [22].
Constrained decoding [72] eliminates structural failures (raises schema validity to 100%) in small LLMs
(0.6B–4B) under Outlines and XGrammar, but a persistent semantic gap remains—schema conformance
does not guarantee content accuracy [72]. XGrammar adds only 4–8ms compilation overhead vs. 2–5s
for Outlines [72]. Constrained decoding can push generation toward locally valid but semantically
incorrect trajectories [72].

6.2 Hallucination Mitigation
Hallucinations arise from stale training data, probabilistic language modeling, and knowledge conflicts
between retrieved evidence and model parameters [59][56]. Key finding: when retrieved evidence
conflicts with LLM internal knowledge, the LLM may ignore the retrieved evidence and produce
hallucinations [56].
DeReC [25] argues that explicit rationale generation may not be needed for high verification
accuracy—dense retrieval + DeBERTa classifier achieves competitive F1 with 92–95% less runtime vs. LLM
rationale pipelines [25]. This trades interpretability for efficiency.
Chain-of-thought prompting with explicit citation of Q-A pairs before label production (SANCTUARY [43]
) improves groundedness. GraphCheck's GNN attention weights provide evidence-level explainability [27]
.
Knowledge conflicts: LLMs with internal knowledge of a claim may "help" verify it without evidence
(zero-evidence GPT-4o achieves 53.2% on AVeriTeC) [7], meaning parametric leakage contaminates even
retrieved-evidence systems.

6.3 Prompt Injection and Untrusted Web Content
This is the most serious unresolved risk for ProofGraph when ingesting web evidence.
Threat severity: OWASP rates prompt injection as LLM01:2025, the top LLM vulnerability [33][36].
Indirect prompt injection occurs when untrusted external content (web pages, files) alters LLM behavior
without user awareness [33]. Five carefully crafted documents can manipulate AI responses 90% of the
time via RAG poisoning [33].
State of defenses (2025–2026): CaMeL (Google DeepMind, March 2025) uses Privileged + Quarantined
LLMs with data provenance tracking, solving 77% of AgentDojo tasks vs. 84% undefended [36]. FIDES
(Microsoft Research, May 2025) applies information-flow control, stopping all attacks in internal
testing [36]. Meta's LlamaFirewall reduced attack success rate by ~90% to 1.75% [36]. MELON (ICML
2025) achieved 0.32% ASR on AgentDojo at double the API calls [36].
However: 12 published defenses were bypassed at >90% success rate by adaptive attacks [36]. The
International AI Safety Report 2026 found sophisticated attackers bypass best-defended models ~50% of
the time with 10 attempts [36]. Anthropic states even adversarially trained Claude Opus 4.5 at ~1% ASR
"still represents meaningful risk" [36]. Prompt injection cannot be fully solved within current LLM
architectures per OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google DeepMind 2025 publications [36].
Practical defenses for ProofGraph: The Dual LLM pattern—a Privileged LLM for trusted pipeline
instructions and a Quarantined LLM without tool access for untrusted web content [37]; Microsoft
Spotlighting (input delimiting, datamarking, encoding, reportedly reducing ASR from >50% to <2%) [37];
Llama Guard for input/output classification [37]; architecture design to minimize blast radius of
successful injection [37]. ClaimCheck's ethics section explicitly acknowledges the risk of inadvertently
retrieving and summarizing low-quality or misleading sources [7].
Note: Neither ClaimCheck [7], VeriFastScore [49], LOKI [22], nor most research systems implement
described defenses against prompt injection from web content—this is an open gap in the field.

Section 7: Rigorous Evaluation, Metrics, and Leakage
7.1 Metrics
Standard classification metrics: Macro-F1 is most widely used [56]. Accuracy can diverge from
macro-F1 by up to 20 points under class imbalance [18]. FEVER score combines verification accuracy with
evidence retrieval accuracy [56]. The FEVEROUS score requires both correct label and adequate evidence
coverage [81].
Evidence retrieval metrics: Recall@k, nDCG@k, B-Pref (incomplete-judgment-aware) [24]. Ev2R recall
(atomic reference-based evidence coverage, threshold 0.44 for AVeriTeC 2025 [4]) is the most rigorous
current standard, replacing the brittle Hungarian METEOR metric [43].
Factuality metrics: FActScore (atomic precision) [5], VeriScore (verifiable claim precision) [53], SAFE
F1 [55], VeriFastScore (correlated 0.87 Pearson with VeriScore) [49]. FACTS Grounding uses LLM
ensemble judging (3 frontier models) [65]. MSE is proposed for ordinal/continuous scoring [11][82].
Decomposition quality: DecMetrics (Completeness, Correctness, Semantic Entropy) [15]; ClaimScore
(supported atomic claims) criticized for missing semantic overlap [15].
Abstention: AbstentionBench uses abstention recall as primary metric (models generally have high
precision, so recall is the bottleneck) [39].
Inter-annotator agreement: RefNLI achieves Fleiss' κ 0.83 [31]; CONFACT Krippendorff's α 0.586 [29];
MMCE entailment α 0.07 (very low) [74]; FactIR Cohen's κ 0.946 [79]. The low agreement on
entailment-based evaluation of extracted claims is a warning for automated evaluation design.

7.2 Benchmark Leakage
This is a critical risk for any evaluation of ProofGraph or its components.
Parametric leakage: LLMs encode training-corpus knowledge of claims. Masking named entities on
AVeriTeC collapses supported-claim recall from 0.421 to 0.131 while overall accuracy drops only 0.03
points—aggregate accuracy hides parametric leakage [57]. Zero-evidence GPT-4o achieves 53.2%
accuracy on AVeriTeC [7]. GPT-4o web search "benefits from temporal data leakage" and cannot be
constrained by publication cutoff dates, frequently retrieving annotated fact-check articles [7].
Temporal leakage: Allowing post-claim evidence raises zero-shot accuracy by 6.3 points on
AVeriTeC [57]. 18.8% of AVeriTeC evidence answers with trusted on-page dates post-date the claim [57].
Live search-engine date filters still return post-cutoff pages [57]. The AVeriTeC 2025 test set fixes a
temporal leakage issue in the 2024 document collection [4].
Data contamination: Evaluation data can enter pre-training corpora and inflate reported
scores [11][57]. VERITAS addresses this with quarterly updates and a held-out private set [82].
Annotation leakage in MultiFC: Evidence often comes from the same fact-checking article that states
the ruling, allowing models to copy rather than verify [57].
Mitigation strategies: Temporal cutoff filtering in retrieval [7][62][40]; holding out private test sets [65];
fresh data collection [11]; the new Ev2R metric requiring evidence coverage not just label
correctness [4][43]; VERITAS's quarterly update commitment [82].

NN-PPI [67] calibrates LLM check-worthiness predictions via nearest-neighbor residual correction from a
small labeled calibration set, without retraining. It improves weighted F1 by up to 33.80% for small
models (Gemma 3 4B) on ClaimBuster [67]. Larger commercial models show near-zero gain (already
saturated) [67]. The system uses all-MiniLM-L6-v2 embeddings indexed in ChromaDB [67]. Standard
post-hoc calibrators (Platt scaling, temperature scaling, isotonic regression) are most effective when
miscalibration is homogeneous; NN-PPI targets input-dependent bias [67].
Confidence scores from different model families are not on a common scale; no standard cross-method
calibration dataset exists [35].

7.4 Human Adjudication
Professional journalists evaluated an automated fact-checking tool and reported only 59% of claims
accurately classified and 58% of retrieved evidence sentences relevant [83]. Inter-rater reliability
between major fact-checking organizations on factual accuracy is very low [62]. Free-text explanations
improve lay users' detection of false information but not journalists' [83]. These findings suggest that
ProofGraph's evaluation must include professional user studies, not just automated metrics.

8.2 Reference Architecture for ProofGraph
Based on the evidence, a production ProofGraph system would minimally include:
1. Input layer: Document/question ingestion
(LLM-CED-style [63] or Claimify-style [6])

sentence segmentation

decontextualization

2. Claim detection: Check-worthiness scoring (ClaimBuster [64] or NN-PPI-calibrated SLM [67])
filtering non-verifiable claims (VeriScore-style [53])
3. Retrieval layer: Hybrid BM25 + dense (FAISS/snowflake-arctic-embed) + sparse neural (SPLADE)
Reciprocal Rank Fusion [24] cross-encoder reranking temporal cutoff enforcement [7][40]
4. Evidence quarantine: Quarantined LLM for untrusted web content processing [37];
Spotlighting/delimiting for injection resistance [37]
5. Verification layer: NLI/verifier (DeBERTa or Bespoke-MiniCheck-7B) for each claim-evidence pair;
confidence scoring; abstention when below threshold [11]
6. Provenance graph: PROV-O-based graph [19] with PROV-CRED enrichment method annotation [35]
; claim nodes, evidence nodes, source nodes, verdict nodes; contradiction links as first-class edges
7. Source credibility: MBFC-style credibility database lookup per source [29]; credibility as edge/node
attribute in provenance graph
8. Report generation: Verdict aggregation per claim (Supported/Contradicted/Insufficient); structured
JSON with provenance pointers; human-readable explanation generation (Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct
style [58])
9. Deployment: Haystack pipelines (serializable, Kubernetes-ready) [70]; FastAPI backend;
PostgreSQL for structured provenance; FAISS/ChromaDB for vector evidence store; Next.js
frontend [80]
Cost estimate (inference): FIRE-style iterative retrieval costs ~$0.01–0.02 per atomic claim [48];
VeriFastScore estimates $1/100 sentences [49]; LOKI estimates $0.001/search via Serper [22].

Section 10: Negative Results, Open Problems, and Opportunities
10.1 Important Negative Results
1. Retrieval degrades accuracy under domain shift: Adding BM25-retrieved evidence decreases LLM
accuracy on ClimateCheck vs. claim-only [18]. Noisy evidence is worse than no evidence for strong
verifiers.
2. Decomposition can hurt stronger verifiers: Decompose-then-verify improves weaker models but
hurts GPT-4o-mini and MiniCheck [76].
3. Abstention worsens with reasoning fine-tuning: RLVR/RLHF reasoning training degrades
abstention ~24% on average; scaling does not help [39].
4. NLI context mismatch: Models produce false entailment/contradiction when evidence refers to a
different entity [31].
5. LLM rationale generation adds hallucination risk: LLM-generated explanations frequently
contain factual inconsistencies and lack grounding [25].
6. Dense retrieval underperforms BM25 on domain-shifted real-world fact-checking [79][18].
7. Adding more evidence hurts via "lost in the middle" [28].
8. No reliable prompt injection defense exists for agentic web-retrieval systems [36].
9. Corpus internal consistency cannot be assumed: ~3.3% of Wikipedia facts contradict other
corpus content; 7.3% of FEVEROUS claims involve corpus-level inconsistencies [71].
10. Inter-annotator agreement on entailment evaluation of extracted claims is very low (α
0.07 [74]), making automated evaluation unreliable for complex claim types.

10.2 Open Problems
• Multilingual and low-resource fact-checking are systematically underexplored [8][56]; most tools
are English-only or English-centric.
• Multimodal claim extraction is largely unexplored [74]; over one-third of debunked claims involve
images and text [74].
• Conflicting evidence and source credibility integration remain immature [29][78]; most systems
assume a single best evidence source.
• Cross-benchmark generalization: the best system on SciFact (macro-F1 0.700) drops to 0.315 on
ClimateCheck [18]; systems do not generalize reliably across domains.
• Calibration across heterogeneous sources: confidence scores from NLI models, LLMs, and KG
retrievers are not on a common scale [35].
• Multi-hop KG retrieval: multi-hop evidence retrieval from KGs is still a major challenge [20]; best
models score 80.32% on multi-hop vs. 93.49% overall on FactKG [14].
• Implicit temporal information: 82% of ChronoFact errors involve claims with implicit temporal
cues [41].
• Explanation quality evaluation: ROUGE inadequately measures reasoning quality in
explanations [12]; entailment-based evaluation has low human agreement [74].
• Retroactive provenance for large KGs: bootstrapping enrichment method records for

• Dynamic leakage prevention: even quarterly-updated benchmarks like VERITAS [82] face the

10.3 Genuinely Novel Opportunities for ProofGraph
The following elements are individually established in research but not integrated in any existing
open-source or commercial system:
1. First-class contradiction link modeling: explicitly representing claim-source disagreements as
typed graph edges (not just labeling each claim independently). No prior system does this [as
analyzed from 29][78][71].
2. Provenance-aware source credibility as a graph property: combining PROV-O/PROV-CRED
enrichment method annotation [35] with real-time source credibility scoring [29][78] in a queryable
provenance graph—ProofGraph could make the credibility of each evidence link explicit and auditable.
3. Confidence-calibrated abstention at claim level: combining NN-PPI calibration [67] with
UNVERIFIABLE abstention [11] and AbstentionBench insights [39] into a per-claim uncertainty output
that feeds into the provenance graph. No existing system provides calibrated uncertainty per claim
with provenance.
4. Cross-corpus consistency checking as a verification step: using CLAIRE-style [71] detection of
internal contradictions within the evidence corpus to flag when evidence for a claim is inconsistent
with other known evidence—treating corpus inconsistency as a first-class verification signal.
5. Temporal claim timeline modeling integrated with provenance: applying ChronoFact's [41]
timeline structure within a provenance graph, linking each claimed event to temporally-indexed
evidence and tracking when contradictions arise in timelines.
6. Journalist-facing explainability with claim-level audit trail: the user study literature [83][62] is
clear that professional fact-checkers need per-step transparency, source selection rationale, and bias
criteria. No current tool provides this at the level ProofGraph envisions. The business case is real:
fact-checking organizations are under severe funding pressure [83][68] and need tools that make
professional workflows faster without replacing editorial judgment.
7. Defense-in-depth prompt injection architecture: implementing the Dual LLM pattern [37]
(Privileged LLM for trusted pipeline instructions, Quarantined LLM for web content) combined with
Spotlighting [37] and taint tracking—no current research fact-checking system describes this in its
architecture, representing a genuine security-first design opportunity.
